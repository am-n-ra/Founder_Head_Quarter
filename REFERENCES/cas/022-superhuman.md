# Superhuman — Growth-Stage Case Study

## Why This Case Matters

Superhuman is the most instructive G1 (PMF Search) case in the startup canon because it shows the *process* of finding PMF, not just the result. Most PMF stories are retrospective ("we had it, it worked"). Superhuman's story is diagnostic: here's how we measured, what we found, and what we changed.

## G1 — Seed: The 22% → 58% Journey

In 2015, Rahul Vohra ran the Sean Ellis survey on Superhuman's early user base. The result: **22% "very disappointed"** — well below the 40% threshold. By conventional wisdom, Superhuman did not have PMF.

But Vohra did something that's become the canonical example of *why you must segment*:

**Step 1: Segment by user type.** Instead of accepting the aggregate score, he broke down responses by user segment. The result: among **email power users** (defined as 100+ inbound messages per day, keyboard shortcut users, tried every email client available), the "very disappointed" score was **58%**. Among everyone else: much lower.

**Step 2: Ignore the majority.** Most product advice says "listen to your users." Vohra did the opposite: he explicitly ignored the 80% of users who scored below 40%, and redesigned Superhuman *exclusively* for the power-user segment that scored 58%.

**Step 3: Double down on what made them love it.** The power users valued speed above all — keyboard-first, zero mouse interaction, instant search, read-receipts for sent email. Vohra cut everything that didn't serve this segment.

## What This Verifies About the Sean Ellis Test

1. **The 40% threshold works — but only if you ask the right people.** Superhuman's aggregate 22% correctly said "no PMF for the general market." The 58% correctly said "strong PMF for a specific segment." The test didn't fail — the interpretation would have failed without segmentation.

2. **Segmentation is not optional.** Superhuman proves that the right question isn't "do we have PMF?" but "for WHOM do we have PMF?" A founder who runs the Sean Ellis test, gets 30%, and gives up — without segmenting — is making the same mistake Vohra avoided.

3. **The "very disappointed" framing works for non-SaaS products.** Superhuman is a subscription product, but the test measures emotional dependence, not payment willingness. Email productivity maps well to the question. (For a marketplace or deep-tech product, the adaptation guidance in Section 22 stands.)

## Limitation of This Case

Superhuman's G2 (repeatable growth) path is not T2D3 — it grew via waitlist scarcity and invite mechanics, not enterprise sales. Its ARR trajectory is closer to a premium consumer model than enterprise SaaS. The G1 lesson stands independently of G2.
