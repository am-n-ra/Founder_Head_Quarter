# Webvan — Growth-Stage Case Study (Counter-Example)

## Why This Case Matters

Webvan is the most-cited premature scaling failure in startup history. Its collapse is the reason "get big fast" has become a cautionary tale rather than a strategy. Every key finding from Startup Genome's research — 70-74% of failures come from premature scaling — is visible in Webvan's trajectory.

## G1 — Seed: PMF Was Never Sought

Webvan's fatal error was not that it failed to achieve PMF — it's that it never looked for it. The company:

- **Built before validating**: Constructed a $35M automated warehouse in Oakland as its first facility, designed to serve the entire San Francisco Bay Area, before proving that enough customers would order groceries online at the required frequency.
- **Scaled before proving**: Expanded to 10 cities (Atlanta, Chicago, Dallas, Los Angeles, etc.) simultaneously while losing money in every single one. Each new city required another $35M warehouse.
- **Never measured retention**: No cohort analysis. No repeat-purchase rate. No NPS. No Sean Ellis survey. The company tracked revenue and order volume but had no idea whether customers came back.
- **Confused IPO success with product success**: The $375M IPO was a financing event, not a validation of the business model. Investors were betting on the dot-com narrative, not on unit economics.

**The damning number**: In 2000, Webvan had $178.5M in revenue against $525.4M in expenses. For every $1 of revenue, it spent $2.94. These are not "growth stage" losses — they are structural unit economics that were never fixed.

## G2 — The Premature Scaling Death Spiral

Webvan's expansion played out exactly like the Startup Genome failure pattern:

1. **Raise large funding** ($830M total, including $375M IPO)
2. **Hire aggressively** (2,000+ employees)
3. **Expand to multiple markets** (10 cities in 18 months)
4. **Build capital-intensive infrastructure** ($35M per warehouse)
5. **Discover unit economics don't work** (each delivery cost more than the revenue it generated)
6. **Run out of cash** (burned $830M in ~4 years)
7. **Collapse** (bankruptcy in July 2001)

The tragedy is that Webvan might have found PMF if it had started small. The concept of grocery delivery was not fundamentally flawed — Instacart (founded 2012) proved that an asset-light model could work. But Webvan never gave itself the chance to iterate because it committed to massive fixed costs before validating demand.

## What This Case Verifies About the Growth Track

1. **Premature scaling is the #1 killer**: Webvan is the textbook case. Scaling spend before PMF guarantees failure — no exceptions.
2. **G1 must precede G2**: Webvan skipped G1 entirely. No amount of capital or technology could compensate.
3. **The IPO is not the finish line**: Webvan's $4.8B IPO valuation was based on narrative, not fundamentals. A public listing at an absurd multiple can kill a company faster than it can save one.
4. **Invariant 1 violation**: The problem (grocery delivery) was a market thesis, not a personal, lived friction. Louis Borders was a bookstore chain cofounder, not someone who personally struggled with grocery delivery.

## Limitation

Webvan is a dot-com era case. The technology landscape (cloud, mobile, gig economy) has changed dramatically. However, the fundamental lesson — validate PMF before scaling fixed costs — is timeless. The pattern has repeated with Better.com (2021), Quibi (2020), and others.
