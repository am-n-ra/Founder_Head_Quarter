# Salesforce — Growth-Stage Case Study

## Why This Case Matters

Salesforce is the company that *defined* the SaaS growth model. Its ARR trajectory from $6M (2001) to $31B (2024) spans the entire growth track — G1 through G4 — over two decades. It's the reference case for T2D3 (the framework was built to describe companies like Salesforce) and for Rule of 40 at scale.

## G1 — Seed: Paying Before the Product Existed

Salesforce's G1 is unusual: Benioff validated demand *before* building, by selling to customers who paid in advance for a product that didn't fully exist yet. The first customers signed contracts based on a demo and Benioff's reputation (Oracle VP, proven enterprise credibility).

**PMF signal**: Pre-paid contracts. When customers commit cash before the product ships, PMF is less a "search" and more a "confirmation."

**Limitation**: This path requires founder credibility (enterprise reputation, network, personal capital). Most founders don't have Benioff's pre-existing positioning. The pre-paid-PMF path is real but only available to people who've already built something before — it's closer to Team-as-Demo (Section 3 variant).

## G2 — T2D3: The Reference Case

Salesforce's revenue progression from 2001 to 2007 ($6M → $500M) was the template Neeraj Agrawal used when he developed the T2D3 framework. Salesforce showed that a SaaS company could grow faster than traditional enterprise software and maintain that growth through multiple doublings.

**What T2D3 gets right about Salesforce**: The shape is correct — early tripling, sustained doubling, clear deceleration as scale increases.

**What T2D3 misses about Salesforce**: Salesforce grew *faster* than T2D3 in its early years (3.7x, 2.4x, 1.85x — the tripling was actually stronger than triple). The framework describes a lower-bound of elite performance, not a ceiling.

## G3 — Rule of 40 at Scale

Salesforce's Rule of 40 journey across 15+ years:

| Period | Growth | Margin | Rule of 40 | Stage |
|--------|--------|--------|------------|-------|
| 2004-2007 | 80-50% | -20% to -5% | 60-45 | Post-IPO hypergrowth |
| 2008-2012 | 40-25% | -5% to +5% | 35-30 | Mid-market expansion |
| 2013-2017 | 30-20% | +5% to +12% | 35-32 | Enterprise dominance |
| 2018-2022 | 25-10% | +12% to +18% | 37-28 | Acquisition-fueled growth |
| 2023+ | ~10% | +20%+ | ~30 | Mature leader |

Through every period, Salesforce's combined score stayed near or above 30. The metric is useful as a *trend* across regimes — not as a binary pass/fail at any single point.

## G4 — Governance and Delegation

Salesforce's governance evolution is textbook:

- **2004 (IPO)**: Board expanded with independent directors. Benioff remained CEO.
- **2013-2018**: Functional leads appointed (CFO, COO, CRO). Benioff still in key deals.
- **2018+**: Benioff stepped back from day-to-day operations. Sales closed without him.
- **2020+**: Acquisition strategy (Slack $27.7B, Tableau $15.7B, MuleSoft $6.5B) run through functional teams, not founder overrides.

The delegation signal (Section 22, G4) is clearly present: by 2018, Salesforce could sell and operate without Benioff in every room.

## What This Case Verifies

1. **T2D3**: Confirmed — but Salesforce shows the framework describes a *baseline* of elite growth, not the ceiling. Real T2D3 companies may exceed the pattern in early years.
2. **Rule of 40**: Confirmed across multiple regimes. The Rule was never below ~28 even in Salesforce's most difficult years. But the trend across regimes matters more than any single quarter.
3. **Delegation signal**: Confirmed — the transition from founder-everything to functional leads is measurable by the point where large deals close without the founder.
4. **Pre-existing credibility as PMF shortcut**: Valid, but marked as available only to founders with established reputations.
